Terms of Use

PharmaSNAP AI · Last updated 5 October 2026

PharmaSNAP AI is not medical advice and it is not a pharmacist. It reads what official documents say about a medicine and reads them back to you. Before you take anything, check with a pharmacist or a doctor. They can look at the real medicine and at your own history. If you feel very unwell, get help straight away. Do not wait for this app.

What the app does

  • You photograph a medicine, a pharmacy label or a health document. The app reads it out in your language and answers your questions about it.
  • You can give it more than one photo (for example a blood test and the medicines you take) and it reads them together in one plain summary.
  • You can see pharmacies near you and open them in Maps. A pharmacy can pay for a labelled slot in that list; it is marked as sponsored and the distances stay real.
  • You can ask whether a medicine is stocked nearby. The app reads the big chains' own public websites and searches the web, and it tells you what a page said and when it said it, with a link. A listing online is not the same as a box on the shelf: stock moves, pages go stale, and the app is reading a website rather than counting what is in the shop. Ring the pharmacy before you drive anywhere.

What the app will not do

These are deliberate limits, not gaps we intend to close quietly.

  • It will not check your medicines for interactions. There is no approved source we can cite for whether two medicines are safe to take together, and we will not guess. Two narrower things do happen, and neither is an interaction check. When two medicines saved in your cabinet contain the same active ingredient, the app tells you so and asks you to check the total. And the app can read what one medicine's own label says about taking it with others. A pharmacist can check your whole list with you.
  • It will not name a loose tablet from a photograph. Too many tablets look alike, and a wrong name is dangerous.
  • It will not diagnose you or choose a medicine for you.

What we quote, and what that means

Every dose and every other number the app gives you has to match a document it actually fetched, such as an approved product label or a public health page, and you can open the sources behind a report. Explanations around those numbers are written in plain words and can be wrong. That is a real protection, and it is not the same as being right about your situation. A document written about a medicine in general was not written about you.

A match is not proof

Many different medicines look alike and packaging changes. What the app shows you can be wrong. Check the name on your own box, and ask a pharmacist if it does not match.
